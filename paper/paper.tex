\begin{abstract}
Mundelein Center, on Loyola University Chicago's Lakeshore Campus, holds 54 general-purpose classrooms on twelve
floors but is served by only two elevator banks: four cars that reach floor~9 and two cars that reach floor~14.
Each class changeover produces long lines. We build a discrete-time simulation of one 15-minute passing period
and use it to compare two low-cost behavioral measures: holding backpacks in front, and walking to and from
floors 2 and~3. We also test whether running floors 10--14 on a time offset spreads the load. In the baseline
configuration, mean waiting time is 5.2 minutes and 23\% of students arrive after class has started. Backpack
compliance cuts mean wait by 31\% and nearly halves lateness; walking removes only 6\% of the wait; together they cut
mean wait by 35\% and lateness to 11\%. Offsetting the upper floors by 5--10 minutes shortens the time to clear
the queue by about 20\% but, combined with the other measures, \emph{raises} the share of late students.
The qualitative ordering of the measures is stable across occupancy, door time, and adjacent-floor traffic. The
absolute numbers are not measurements: every timing parameter is an assumption, and we close with the timing study
needed to calibrate the model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Mundelein Center is a tall, narrow classroom building. Its classrooms are spread from floor~1 to floor~14, and
all of them are reached through two elevator banks of four and two cars. Only the two-car bank travels above
floor~9. Every 75 minutes on Tuesdays and Thursdays and every 50 minutes on Mondays, Wednesdays, and
Fridays, students leaving one class and arriving for the next converge on the elevators. Cars leave the upper
floors carrying four to six people and still stop at every floor below, even though they are physically full,
so students on the lower floors wait for cars that cannot take them.

We focus on classes that end before 4~PM and on two behavioral measures that cost nothing to implement: asking
students to take their backpacks off and hold them in front (our estimate: two more riders per car), and asking
students on floors 2 and~3 to walk. A third question is scheduling: because the registrar's grid fixes the
class times, can the upper floors run on a shifted clock so that their peak does not coincide with the rest of
the building?

The contribution is a transparent, reproducible model, not a measurement; the classical treatments of elevator traffic~\cite{barney,strakosch} motivate its structure. All code, parameters, and result
tables accompany this paper (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).

\subsection{The building and its classrooms}

building cleanly into a lower group (floors 1--7, 45 rooms) and an upper group (floors 10--14, 9 rooms) that only

Floor~1 is an atrium lobby, and the stairs from floor~1 to floor~2 feel like two ordinary flights. Students
leave the building through the lobby.

\subsection{The scheduling grid}

The Registrar's standard grid~\cite{luc-grid} defines seven 50-minute Monday--Wednesday--Friday blocks (8:15 to
3:35) and five 75-minute Tuesday--Thursday blocks (8:30 to 3:45) before the evening blocks, which start at
4:15 and are outside the scope of this study. Every consecutive pair of blocks, in either pattern, is separated
by a 15-minute passing period. Our simulation models that period.

Classes seat 30--35 students, but enrollment is often 15--25. Elevator cars are rated for 3{,}000~lb, which is
roughly 17--20 riders by weight; observed loads are four to six, so space, not weight, is the binding limit.

\section{Model}

\subsection{Demand}

One run simulates a single passing period. Time zero is the nominal end of the outgoing class and
$T=900$ seconds is the nominal start of the incoming class. Each classroom holds an outgoing and an incoming class
independently with probability $p_{occ}$; each class has between 15 and 25 students, uniformly at random.
Leavers depart uniformly over the first two minutes. A fraction $q$ of leavers go on to another class in the
building (assigned to an incoming room with probability proportional to remaining seats, and to an adjacent
floor with probability $a$); the rest leave via the lobby. The seats in incoming rooms that transfers do not fill
are occupied by students who arrive from the lobby with a triangular distribution over the ten minutes before
class start, peaking three minutes before.

\subsection{Elevators}

Cars are simulated in one-second steps. The four-car bank serves floors 1--9 and the two-car bank floors 1--14.
Passengers whose trip includes a floor above 9 can use only the two-car bank. Cars follow a collective rule:
continue in the current direction while there are riders to drop or calls ahead; otherwise reverse or go idle.
Idle cars go to the nearest unclaimed call. Crucially, cars do \emph{not} skip a floor when full: a car stops for
any call in its direction of travel and then boards as many waiting people as it has room for. This reproduces the
empty stops observed in practice. A car's capacity for a trip is drawn uniformly from $\{4,5,6\}$, and with
backpack compliance $b$ it is increased by two with probability $b$.

Timing assumptions are 3 seconds between floors (6 seconds from floor~1 to~2), 8 seconds of fixed door time per
stop, and 1 second per rider boarding or alighting. These are assumptions, not measurements.

\subsection{Behavioral measures and the offset}

\emph{Walking.} A rider whose trip starts on floor 2 or 3 and goes down walks with probability $w_d$ (0.8 in the
main experiments). A rider going up to floor 2 walks with probability 0.5 and to floor~3 with probability 0.2,
reflecting the atrium. Walkers take 20 seconds per flight (40 for the atrium) and do not use the elevators.
\emph{Backpacks.} The compliance parameter $b$ above, set to 1 in the main experiments. \emph{Offset.}
Classes on floors 10--14 start and end $o$ minutes earlier than the grid ($o\in\{0,5,10\}$), preserving their
length. A student moving from a lower floor to an upper floor therefore has $15-o$ minutes between classes.

\subsection{Metrics}

For each run we record the mean and 90th-percentile wait (from when a rider is ready to when the rider boards),
the share of students who reach their next class after it starts (walkers included), the share of stops at
which nobody boards or alights, and the time to clear all queues. All figures average 60 replicates with
different random seeds; we report standard errors of the mean for wait and lateness.

\section{Results}

The main experiments use $p_{occ}=0.4$ (about 570 elevator riders per changeover), $q=0.6$, and $a=0.2$.
We chose $p_{occ}$ so that the baseline reproduces the qualitative description of a long line before every
class without collapse; Section~\ref{sec:occ} shows how the results change with occupancy.

Table~\ref{tab:policy} and Figure~\ref{fig:wait} show the headline result. Holding backpacks in front reduces
mean wait by 31\% and nearly halves the share of late students. Walking is much weaker: it removes only about
7\% of elevator riders, because floors 2 and~3 hold 9 of the 54 rooms and only partial compliance is
realistic on the way up. Together the two measures reduce mean wait by 35\%, to 3.4 minutes, and lateness
from 22.9\% to 11.0\%. The effect of backpacks is nearly linear in compliance: each additional 25\% of
compliance removes roughly 20--30 seconds of mean wait (Figure~\ref{fig:comp}).

\subsection{Where the waiting happens}

Waits are not uniform across the building (Figure~\ref{fig:floor}). Floor~10, the first floor served only by the
two-car bank, is the worst: riders there wait about 10 minutes in the baseline and 7.5 minutes with both
measures. Floors 5--7 are next, at about 5--6 minutes. The measures help everywhere but do not remove the upper-floor
bottleneck, which comes from the structure of the banks.

An offset trades one problem for another (Table~\ref{tab:offset}, Figures~\ref{fig:wait} and~\ref{fig:late}).
A 10-minute offset shortens the mean wait by about 9\% in the baseline and 12\% with both measures, and shortens
the time to clear the queue by 22\% and 20\%. It does not reduce lateness: in the baseline the change is within
noise, and with both measures lateness \emph{increases} from 11.0\% to 14.4\%, because students moving from a
lower floor to an upper one now have only five minutes. Students moving between adjacent floors are
unaffected, since the group boundary falls between floors 7 and 10, which have no classrooms in between;
the cost falls entirely on students changing between the lower and upper groups. A 5-minute offset gives
about half of the reduction in clearing time at about half of the added lateness, so we regard it as the more defensible choice if the
registrar would consider off-grid start times.

\subsection{Sensitivity}
\label{sec:occ}

\paragraph{Occupancy.} The absolute waits depend strongly on how many rooms are in use
(Figure~\ref{fig:occ}): from 2.6 minutes at 20\% occupancy to 17 minutes at full occupancy in the baseline.
The relative benefit of backpacks plus walking is stable, between 29\% and 36\% across the whole range.
At 20\% occupancy the baseline already has 7.9\% late students, rising to 62\% at full occupancy.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{fig_occupancy_sweep.pdf}
\caption{Mean wait as room occupancy grows.}
\label{fig:occ}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Door time.} Doubling the door time per stop from 5 to 12 seconds raises the baseline mean wait from 236
to 432 seconds, while the relative benefit of both measures stays between 34\% and 36\%. Door time matters
because every empty stop costs a full door cycle; this is the direct cost of the lack of a full-car bypass.

\paragraph{Adjacent-floor transfers.} Varying the share of transfers that go to an adjacent floor from 10\% to 30\%
changes the late share by only 1--2.5 points in either direction (Figure~\ref{fig:adj}), because without stairs the
adjacent-floor riders still use the elevators. Offering stairs for one-floor trips, an extension beyond the
two measures requested, reduces the combined-policy mean wait from 196 to 176 seconds
(about 10\%) at an adjacent share of 30\% and 80\% stair use, and the late share from 10.4\% to 9.1\%.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Backpacks matter most.} The cars leave full because of volume, not weight. Making room for two more
riders per car is by far the largest single lever in the model, and it costs nothing but a message. The
model also shows that it scales smoothly: partial compliance gives partial benefit.

\paragraph{Walking is a supporting measure.} Floors 2 and 3 are only 17\% of the classrooms, and the
atrium makes the climb to floor~2 feel like two flights. The gain comes mostly from the way down: walkers
remove the empty stops on floors 3 and 2 that full cars make on their way to the lobby.

\paragraph{The upper floors need structural help.} Floor~10 is the worst floor even after both measures,
because all of its traffic goes through the two-car bank. Behavioral measures soften the problem; moving some of
the load off the upper floors in the room assignment, or offsetting their clock, would act on the cause.
The offset is a trade-off, not a free gain.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No measurements.} Every timing parameter (travel, door, boarding) and every behavioral
share (leaving spread, arrival distribution, transfer share, compliance) is an assumption. The model
produces \emph{relative} comparisons we believe are reasonable; absolute minutes and percentages should not be
quoted as facts about Mundelein Center.
\item \textbf{One passing period.} We simulate one changeover from an empty building. Backlog from the previous
changeover would make the baseline worse.
\item \textbf{Uniform schedule.} Without a room-by-block schedule we assume every room is in use with probability
$p_{occ}$ in every block and cannot distinguish heavy blocks from light ones.
\item \textbf{Simple dispatching.} The real controller is proprietary and may behave differently. We deliberately
did not model a full-car bypass or down-peak mode, because these were out of scope.
\item \textbf{Static students.} Students do not adapt by leaving earlier when they see a line, which would
reduce lateness in practice.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions and recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Ask students to hold backpacks in front. In the model this is the largest single improvement: about
31\% less mean wait and half the late arrivals at full compliance.
\item Ask students on floors 2 and 3 to walk, especially downward. It is a smaller gain, about 6\% of wait on its
own, but it combines with the first measure, and the two together reduce mean wait by about 35\%.
\item If the registrar will consider it, try a 5-minute offset for floors 10--14. It shortens the time to clear
the queue noticeably, but it costs students who move between floor groups, so measure before extending it.
\item Do not expect behavioral measures alone to fix floor~10. Its waiting time is structural.
\item Before quoting any number, run a timing study. Time round trips from floor~9, count stops and riders, and
measure the door time per stop.
\end{enumerate}
